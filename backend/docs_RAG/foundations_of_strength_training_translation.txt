BASICS OF BODY MECHANICS
A human consists of 206 bones and 450-500 muscles that constantly work together so we can move, breathe, look, pump blood, and generally stay alive.
In this chapter, we will cover the greatest basics of our body's mechanics, which will allow us to ground our understanding of how our muscles work together with the forces that act upon them.

[Page 7]
THE LEVER
The movements of our body are produced through a system of levers. These levers work together to create coordinated movements. Some levers work dynamically (moving the body), others statically (stabilizing the body).
The simplest lever that exists is a force applied to an arm that rotates around an axis of rotation. A resistance force also acts on this same arm on the same side of the axis of rotation, "fighting" against the applied force.

An ideal example of such a lever (unilateral) is doing a bicep curl with a dumbbell.
On a long lever, a force (the dumbbell) is applied to our forearm. On a short lever, our bicep works mainly to counteract this force - so the dumbbell and our forearm don't drop. The axis of rotation of the lever becomes our elbow joint.
This is a third-class lever. Most levers in our body work exactly this way. It is often said that someone has a favorable lever system. This means they are suitably built (e.g., have short forearm bones) to lift heavy weights.

[Page 8]
TYPES OF FORCES

Compressive
This is a force acting, for example, on our spine when we hold a barbell on our shoulders before a squat. This force compresses the vertebrae together, bringing them closer to each other. It is a very good force - strengthening our joints.

Tensile
This force appears in our body when, for example, we hang on a pull-up bar. How does it work? Let's look at our vertebrae again. It's the opposite of compressive force. It can also be called a decompressive force.

Shear
A force acting, for example, on our knee when we extend our leg while sitting. An upward force acts on our femur, while a downward force acts on our tibia (lower leg). This creates a shear force on our knee joint. It can be destructive to knees, especially under heavy loads, and should be avoided or at least reduced to a minimum in the training process.

Torsional
Torsion is a force that rotates an object (twists it). For example, when we look to the side, we create torsional forces in our cervical vertebrae. In training, the best example would be performing torso twists under a load.

[Page 9]
TYPES OF MUSCLE CONTRACTIONS

Concentric
This is a muscle contraction that leads to its shortening and thus bringing two bones closer together at the axis of rotation of a given joint. In the case of compound exercises, it will cause the weight to be lifted.

Eccentric
The opposite of concentric - it moves two bones away from each other. In a compound movement, this will be the lowering of the weight.

Isometric
Despite high muscle tension, the muscle does not change its length and the body position does not change significantly at any time. It's impossible for nothing to move at all, but it won't be visible to the naked eye.

Isotonic
Muscle tension that remains unchanged throughout its duration. This can only happen with constant external tension at every moment of the movement. This is impossible with free weights, and only possible on machines designed to ensure the force acts on our body at a 90-degree angle throughout the entire movement. In practice, this isn't 100% possible, but it's conventionally used to describe the contraction in an exercise like "machine flyes".

Isokinetic
A contraction that can only be produced on a specially designed machine for testing strength at different joint angles. The movement is performed at a constant, unvarying speed.

TRAINING
We often say "I'm going to training", "I'm training at the gym", or "real training starts when you want to stop" if we are a typical Insta personal trainer. In any case, let's ask ourselves a question: what is training? Are we really training or just exercising?
Training is a process consisting of subjecting the body to gradually increasing loads, resulting in adaptation and an increase in the level of specific motor traits. The concept of training also includes learning movement habits associated with a given sport discipline. Through appropriate training combined with proper nutrition, you can also shape certain morphological features, e.g., increase muscle mass or reduce body fat levels.

I don't intend to delve into sports theory or training theory right now, but if you read with understanding, at this point you probably already know - whether you are exercising or training. A person who is training is one who has a training plan with a scheduled progression, sticking to this schedule over a certain period of time. If you come in and have to do "biceps" or "chest" and choose random exercises to get a pump, and don't pay attention to weekly progressive overload, you cannot call it training. Of course, it may happen that by just exercising, we achieve better results, but we don't control the process and we don't even know what stimulus our body is receiving.

[Page 11]
TRAINING VARIABLES
These are parameters that we can variously moderate and juggle to maintain continuous progress in our training. We have volume, frequency, intensity, tempo, range of motion, and tension here. It's worth sticking to a rigid plan at the beginning and controlling only the load, but it won't hurt to familiarize yourself with these elementary parameters.

[Page 12]
Volume = weight x number of reps x number of sets x range of motion
For example: if we perform a bench press of 100 kg for 5 sets of 5 repetitions, our volume will be calculated as follows: 100 x 5 x 5 = 2500 kg.
The range of motion is thrown in here for awareness that it is important, but we don't calculate it in the equation. The point is that if we increase the volume but the range of motion is smaller, e.g., we press halfway instead of a full movement, it will not be progress.

The volume of our training is a value we want to increase as our experience grows. It does not necessarily have to increase from workout to workout or from week to week. Ultimately, however, we want to see that we have moved forward every few months.

TRAINING LOAD AND REPETITIONS
| TRAINING ZONE | GOAL | LOAD |
| 1-3 Reps | Power and muscle strength | Very heavy |
| 3-7 Reps | Muscle strength | Heavy |
| 8-12 Reps | Muscle strength and endurance | Moderate |
| 13-25 Reps | Muscle endurance | Light |

Intensity – by intensity we mean the percentage of maximum weight in resistance training. It is often confused with density (which is the amount of work done in a unit of time. Dense training is one with short breaks and a large number of sets in a short time).
On the example of the bench press once again: If our maximum weight we can press is 200 kg, breaking it down into intensity percentages:
100% = 200 kg
90% = 180 kg
80% = 160 kg
70% = 140 kg etc.
It is not true that low-intensity training cannot be hard. If we put on 50% of the maximum weight and do a set to failure, it will be very hard.
The second version of intensity is the RPE scale, i.e., to what extent we approached failure with a given weight.

[Page 13]
RPE SCALE

2 - VERY EASY
3 - EASY
4 - COMFORTABLE
5 - SOMEWHAT HARD

10 - MAXIMAL

[Page 14]
Frequency - this is simply the number of training units in a given time frame. A good frequency is training every roughly 48/72 hours. This gives us a frequency of training a given muscle group three times a week. To maximize the anabolic response of our muscles, this frequency, about three times a week, will be optimal. As you will see later in the point about adaptation, these frequency needs change, which does not mean that you have to train more often or less often - you just have to manipulate other variables more to adapt to these needs. But frequency can also be changed depending on the specific training period we are in.

[Page 15]
Range of motion - the greater the range of motion through which we are able to train muscles under tension, the greater the stimulus for growth we will provide them. As you read earlier, this is one of the guidelines for calculating volume, so it is worth taking care that this range of motion is as large as possible - of course, as I already mentioned, under tension. Let's remember that when the lever is 0, the range of motion ceases to be important. We always need at least minimal tension for the movement we perform to be work under load.

[Page 16]
Tension - we can lift really heavy weights, but we need tension in our muscles to stimulate them. A perpendicular force applied relative to the lever arm will provide us with exactly this tension. It's easy to feel this yourself by touching the muscle you want to check if it's under tension while doing an exercise. Here I just wanted to talk about tension briefly so that you are aware that it is an important training variable.

[Page 17]
Tempo - is the least important variable here, but nevertheless important. It can be heavily manipulated. Beginners, however, should not focus much on the concept of tempo. In your awareness, definitely keep enough to always control the eccentric movement - so the barbell doesn't just fall. This will also help us maintain muscle tension in both phases. An additional important element in tempo is the so-called "pause". In many exercises, it can turn out to be a nice addition. E.g.: bench press, squat, overhead press. In any case, as long as we are beginners, let's not focus on tempo beyond controlling the eccentric phase.

Tempo is often described by a 4-digit pattern:
Example: 4-3-2-1
4 - time of lowering the weight in seconds (eccentric phase)
3 - time spent in maximum stretch in seconds
2 - time of lifting the weight in seconds (concentric phase)
1 - time spent in maximum contraction

ADAPTATION
The work done in training inflicts stress on our body, after which it must recover. To move forward, we must inflict a stress that will disrupt homeostasis and cause supercompensation. Supercompensation is the rebuilding/overbuilding of our muscles or energy reserves or other traits so that the next time the body is prepared for such stress. That's why we need constant progression, because the body adapts and if we just repeat what we did, it will no longer trigger supercompensation. Fortunately, there is also something like the biological law of accommodation, which has its pros and cons. The downside is that we get used to it and have to take care of progression in the long run. But the plus is that accommodation does not happen after one workout. Probably the same stimulus is able to induce supercompensation for a few weeks before it completely loses its meaning. However, as long as we are beginners, it's worth taking advantage of the fact that we develop quickly and trying to add weight every workout.

[Page 19]
PROGRESSION
How to deal with progression at every stage of advancement? Our adaptive capabilities begin to slow down over time, especially as we approach our genetic limit. It may surprise someone, but after just two years of a solid training process, if we do not use doping, we approach our limit. It can be said that we then achieve 90% of what we are able to build. When we are beginners, progression looks in such a way that each subsequent time we add weight. For example, we do 40 kg for 5 sets of 5 repetitions in the squat. On the next squat workout, we do 45 kg for 5 sets of 5 repetitions. On the next one, 50 kg 5 x 5. In later stages of our career, this time to add weight will lengthen. The training layout will also change, as we will be able to add weight more slowly.

[Page 20]
Let's divide our progression stages into:

Beginner
As we discussed in the first slide - we progress here from workout to workout. Without a problem, every 48 hours we are fully recovered and ready for a larger stimulus thanks to supercompensation. This is the most pleasant stage of training, when we see effects every time we enter the training room. This stage is best utilized by training the whole body every 48 hours, but to train safely and have order in the week, I recommend one 72h break. This will give us three full-body workouts a week. In the third segment of the book, you will find out what training will be best suited for this.

Intermediate
When we reach a stage where we are unable to add weight every workout, we have to jump to a plan in which a given stimulus will be increased every week. We can do this by dividing our training during the week into strength, hypertrophy, and strength endurance.
Thanks to such a division, we can train every muscle group three times a week, giving it a completely different stimulus, and maintain frequency even though adaptive capabilities have slowed down.

[Page 21]
Advanced
When we are already advanced, we have to look much more broadly at our training. There are no longer day-to-day or even week-to-week changes. We will need a very long time for adaptation to occur. We also have to manipulate our training variables over a period of a month or even longer. For example: starting with high volume and low intensity, and ending completely opposite - high intensity and low volume. When we reach this stage, every next step is already very hard and very few people are able to move forward at this level - it requires a lot of self-denial and generally health!

HYPERTROPHY
Muscle growth in our body is called hypertrophy or hyperplasia. Hypertrophy (the more important one) is simply the enlargement of our muscle cells. It is divided into myofibrillar hypertrophy, i.e., the growth of muscle fibers, and sarcoplasmic, i.e., the growth of glycogen stores and everything that surrounds the myofibrils in the cell.
Why is this important to us? - it's the growth of our muscles, so something worth achieving, since we put so much work into lifting iron. It's nice if it's relatively visible. Hyperplasia, on the other hand, is the creation of new cells through which the muscle as a whole increases in size. As of today, there is no scientific evidence for muscle growth through hyperplasia in humans.

[Page 23]
How do we achieve such hypertrophy? There are three mechanisms that I will briefly describe:
MECHANICAL TENSION + METABOLIC STRESS + MUSCLE DAMAGE -> MUSCLE GROWTH

Mechanical tension is a combination of a perpendicular force produced by a weight with the stretching of this muscle. In layman's terms - the larger the weight (for the muscle) carried through the larger the range of motion (under tension) - the better the hypertrophic effect.

Metabolic stress is the accumulation of metabolites in the muscle. The greater the metabolic stress, the longer we deprive our muscle tissue of oxygen. We will accumulate the most of this stress by doing a set to failure with dropsets and holding them in isometrics. The most important compounds that accumulate are: lactate, hydrogen ions, and inorganic phosphate. This causes: an increase in the number of muscle fibers activated, a greater hormonal release, and swelling of muscle cells. It's worth remembering that this is a less important mechanism than mechanical tension.

Muscle damage - training with appropriate intensity (RPE) causes slight damage to the fibers that worked. It used to be thought that only rebuilding this damage causes hypertrophy. First of all, it's not necessary, and secondly, it's the least important of all three mechanisms. Excessive muscle damage can be dangerous, as plain logic tells us. For this reason, static stretching should be avoided after strength training.

[Page 24]
STRUCTURAL BALANCE
This is the appropriate balance of forces between muscles acting oppositely on the same joint or body segment. You can disrupt this balance by developing the strength of individual muscle groups to a greater extent - and this can happen even if it is not intentional!
Such an imbalance of forces leads to an increased risk of injury to the part that is too weak relative to its opposite. The simplest scheme we should follow when it comes to these force balances looks like this:

Calf muscles : tibial muscles - 3:1
Elbow flexors : elbow extensors - 1:1
Hip flexors : hip extensors - 1:1
Knee flexors : knee extensors - 2:3
Internal shoulder rotators : external shoulder rotators - 3:2
Arm flexors : arm extensors - 2:3
Trunk flexors : trunk extensors - 1:1

TYPES OF EXERCISES
Isolation exercises are those that will activate only one lever for dynamic work. So, for example, the work will be done only in the elbow - biceps/triceps. When we perform the aforementioned "biceps curl", it will be an isolation exercise. We can also train the biceps with a compound exercise, such as a pull-up or row - then the work occurs in the elbow and shoulder joints. Our body likes to work as a team and most of our training should be precisely compound exercises. Isolation exercises should be treated more as an addition - to polish a weak body part.
To sum it up nicely - isolation exercises are those in which the weight moves in a circle (around the axis of rotation of the joint), while compound exercises are those in which several levers work at once, and the weight moves in a straight line.

POSTURE DEFECTS
I wrote two theses on the subject of posture defects - my bachelor's and master's degrees - but I still feel I know very little. I examined posture defects in non-gym-goers and compared their posture with people who train. In my master's studies, however, I compared those same gym-goers after two years of training and checked how their posture had changed. After checking the results I say: it is worth training (intelligently!).
Before you start reading this chapter, I want you to know, Dear Viewer, that posture defects are road signs for a training plan. You must be aware (or at least your trainer) of your posture defects - otherwise, you can really hurt yourself. In this chapter, I will try to outline the most popular ones so that you can judge for yourself whether you might have one of them. I encourage everyone starting an adventure with the gym to go to a physiotherapist for a posture assessment. And especially if you notice any of these defects - run there as soon as possible.

[Page 27]
Flat back / posterior pelvic tilt
Reduction of the curvature of the lower spine. This posture is characterized by shortened/too strong hip extensors (glutes and hamstrings), abdominal muscles, and too loose (lengthened) hip flexors (those muscles under the belly). It is a much rarer problem than anterior pelvic tilt, but definitely worth keeping in mind.
Correction:
Stretching: abs, glutes, hamstrings
Strengthening: hip flexors
Exercises: hanging leg raises

Hyperlordosis / anterior pelvic tilt
The pelvis is tilted forward and down. The spine in the lumbar region is exaggeratedly arched. Hip flexors and spinal erectors are shortened/too strong. Abdominal muscles, hamstrings, glutes are relaxed/too weak. This strongly increases the chance of lower back injuries, especially when training with weights.
Correction:
Stretching: hip flexors, spinal erectors
Strengthening: glutes, abs, hamstrings
Exercises: couch stretch, Hip Thrust, lying leg curls, plank

[Page 28]
Hyperkyphosis
Kyphosis is the natural curvature of the thoracic spine. Its deepening can cause us a lot of problems. Hyperkyphosis gives us a hunched posture, often combined with shoulder protraction (more on that later). It can strongly limit the ability to press overhead or any other exercises where the arms go up.
Correction:
Stretching: flexors of the thoracic section (rectus abdominis, obliques)
Strengthening: extensor muscles of the thoracic section (spinal erectors, quadratus lumborum, trapezius - descending part, i.e., lower)
Exercises: T-raise, facepull, Superman, Y-raise

Forward head posture
Head protruding in front of the chest, hanging neck - these are signs of head protraction - a very common problem caused mainly by human development. We slouch in front of computers, look at phones, stare at books in school. We constantly provoke an incorrect position of our head. We can also hurt ourselves while sleeping. If we sleep on our backs and put a pillow under our heads, verify whether we are stretching the muscles at the back of our neck for 6-10 hours a day, which should be loose.
Correction:
Strengthening: extensors of the cervical spine (back of the neck)
Stretching: flexors of the cervical section (front of the neck)
Exercises: isometric retraction (lying down), head retraction while lying on the stomach

[Page 29]
Winged scapulae
A common problem in young people - when the inferior angle of the scapula (the medial part at the bottom) sticks out strongly from the spine on both sides. It often combines with shoulder protraction. This problem greatly increases the risk of injury due to: a weakened supraspinatus muscle and weak external rotation of the arm. Shoulder flexion and external rotation can be severely limited by such a scapula position.
Here we also have a definitely weakened serratus anterior muscle. Probably the rhomboid muscle is weakened, while the pectoralis minor is too strong.
Correction:
Stretching: pectoralis minor, lats
Strengthening: serratus anterior, rhomboids, supraspinatus
Exercises: all kinds of rows, Push Up Plus, high-5 stretching, Pullover

Shoulder protraction
Gorilla posture - it is easy to acquire it at the gym too if we don't watch our structural balance. We need to use push and pull exercises equally. When push starts to dominate heavily - as is often seen among gym-goers often wearing flip-flops, you can often see this beautifully cultivated gorilla posture. How to counteract this when it already concerns us?

[Page 30]
Correction:
Stretching: subscapularis, biceps, pectoralis major and minor
Strengthening: trapezius, rhomboids, external rotation
Exercises: all kinds of rows (must be predominant), Scap Pull-Up, Face Pull

Scoliosis
There isn't much we can do specifically with scoliosis in training.
The important thing is what we must not do: up to 20 degrees of curvature, doctors generally allow for axial loading of the spine. Above 20 degrees, we train at our own risk, and strictly speaking, it shouldn't be done. You can, of course, still train without axial loading (with spinal compression, e.g., squats, etc.). If you notice a lateral curvature or a hump on either side of the spine when bending forward, be sure to go for a check-up. You will find out how big a curvature you have and what you can train so as not to worsen this condition, halt the progression of the defect, and protect yourself against future problems.

KINEMATIC CHAIN
By the kinematic chain in our body, we mean all the segments activated to perform a specific movement. The longer the chain, the more difficult the exercise is in terms of stabilization. A chain can also be open or closed. An open chain is one where the limbs move freely, you could say "without the floor," so, for example, a bench press would be an example of an open chain chest exercise. Meanwhile, a push-up on the floor will be an exercise in a closed kinematic chain. Another example - a squat. Closed chain, but leg extensions on a machine will be an open chain. An example of a long closed chain working in a given exercise would be the standing overhead press - from the feet to the hands, the whole body is activated and forced to work. Conversely, lateral raises performed lying sideways on a bench will have a very short chain (open) - the working chain is only from the hand to the chest.
This is not the most crucial knowledge in training, but it builds body awareness well and makes us realize which exercises are more complex than others.

VALSALVA MANEUVER
Breath - such an important thing in our lives. Without breath, basically, there would be no life. And without life, there would be no breath. Getting pretty philosophical, huh? But philosophy aside: in training, breathing is just as important as in life. I just wanted to emphasize this strongly with that beautiful poem. In fitness, they often say, inhale on the way down and exhale on the way up (e.g., in a squat). Here, it's the opposite! In resistance training, we need a lot of stabilization. Breath can help us with this if we use it correctly. There is a medical practice called the Valsalva maneuver, during which we take in a large amount of air, close the glottis so the air cannot escape, and thanks to this we have much greater stabilization and strength capabilities. For example, women use such a maneuver during pushing in childbirth. The most important thing is to draw air into the belly and chest, not into the mouth. This will increase intra-abdominal pressure. Adding to this the tension of the abdominal muscles - even better if we wear a weightlifting belt - we will create a powerful stabilizing force for our torso.
This may seem dangerous, but it is precisely this maneuver that protects both our heart and brain by increasing intrathoracic pressure. We take a breath before each repetition. We hold the air in our torso through the concentric and eccentric phases. When we return to the starting point, we exhale. And before the next repetition, we again take in air and close the glottis.

TYPES OF GRIPS
We have several ways to grip a barbell/cable handle/pull-up bar or any other equipment. Each of them offers us slightly different pros and cons. Let's take a look at them!

First of all: A HEALTHY ONE! Whichever grip we choose. The most important thing is not to overload the wrists. To make it more comfortable for them during pressing movements - we should rest our hand on the barbell/dumbbell/whatever as close to the base of the palm as possible. We can achieve this by slightly rotating the hand so that the equipment crosses the base diagonally. The shorter this distance, the lower the wrist overload, and the greater the ability to transfer force from other muscles to the weight.

[Page 34]
Overhand grip (Pronated)
An overhand grip is a hand position covering the equipment from above. The palm faces our body. This grip is most often used for most barbell presses and when doing wider grip pull-ups. Often in deadlifts, we also grip the barbell this way, but there is also a variation with a hook grip - when the weights get very heavy and we still want to use this grip.

Underhand grip (Supinated)
The palm faces away from our body. We grab the equipment from below. This grip is rarely used for pressing unless we want to increase the activation of the upper chest. Personally, however, I think there are better ways to do this. When we perform barbell rows or pull-ups with a shoulder-width grip, this grip will be optimal for us.

[Page 35]
Hammer grip (Neutral)
The hammer grip is the strongest if we pull up or row. Also in pressing, if we have the opportunity, it is the most comfortable for our shoulders. It should not be confused with a neutral grip, which is a slightly different position of our arm.

Alternating/Mixed grip
Another type of grip used mostly in deadlifts. It is a combination of an underhand and an overhand grip - each hand differently. It is the strongest form of gripping a barbell, thanks to the fact that one hand, as it opens, presses the barbell into the other hand, so they mutually clamp down throughout the exercise. Unfortunately, it carries many dangers. The first is a high risk of tearing the biceps. You often see videos on the internet of this happening - exactly during a deadlift. Even slight tension in the biceps of the arm holding the barbell underhand is enough to cause a problem. Thus, 100% motor control is necessary not to make even a minimal movement in the elbow joint, which is difficult. The second problem is a muscular imbalance of the upper back and rotation. One arm is in external rotation, the other in internal rotation. This sets the back muscles in different positions and can cause muscle imbalances that become troublesome over time.

ANATOMICAL PLANES
Our body in motion is divided into three intersecting planes. We perform exercises exactly within these planes, and we should take care to maintain balance in their respective sections into which they divide the body, i.e.:
- left and right (sagittal plane),
- front and back (frontal/coronal plane),
- top and bottom (transverse plane).
I want you to be aware of this division, because in subsequent chapters I will certainly use phrases like "in the sagittal plane" - then we will understand each other well. Besides, I think this is very valuable information for general understanding of our body.

HIP THRUST
I hope you'll forgive me, gentlemen, but I think it's fitting to start with the ladies – that's how it seems to me. Well, we have before us the absolute guru when it comes to developing those magnificences that each of us admires - women's glutes (now you'll definitely forgive me). Hence, in the training for beginners - version for women, this exercise is included. But don't get me wrong. The entire back of our body will have a lot of work here, so it is a very important exercise in the foundation series. There is no other exercise that, in such a large range of motion, has such overload potential and practically isolates the glutes in action. Thus, ladies who do not necessarily want to build up their thighs will also find solace in this lift. But just so you don't think I've forgotten about you, guys - for athletes sprinting, jumping, and for those who want to improve the upper phase of their deadlift - this will also be an ideal exercise. Convinced? Let's go!

[Page 39]
1. Preparing the body to perform the exercise will be totally different in hip thrusts compared to the subsequent exercises you will (I hope) read about further. The head, the sequence of tensions, and generally the position of the body and the barbell mean that we must learn new movement patterns. We perform them by resting the area around our shoulder blades on a platform, keeping our feet on the floor. We form a perfect perpendicular stool out of our body. But step by step - just like building a house, here we will start from the bottom up.

2. We set our feet parallel, hip-width apart - this will give us the best base of support - the most stable position. This is not essentially obligatory. Glutes are built very differently, and some people may find that they feel their work much better with their toes spread wider, and even with their feet spread wider. Therefore, initially, I would suggest such a stable version, but over time it won't hurt to experiment, to feel where your sweet spot is in the setup - it's quite similar with the squat, but more on that later because there are other factors that decide on the feet there. We already know how it should work in the frontal and transverse planes. And what about the sagittal plane, i.e., how far from our hips should the feet be?

3. We will check this only during the actual thrust of the barbell upwards - we need exactly perpendicular lines of the lower legs to the torso with the thighs and to the floor. So we want a perfect vertical formed from our shins when we have full hip extension - when our thighs and torso are a horizontal straight line. Once we find this position, we can go back down and think through the next points of our body sequentially upwards. I will also add that if the feet are too far away - this will increase the work of the back of our thighs (hamstrings - semimembranosus and semitendinosus), whereas if the feet are too close to our hips - this will increase the work of our quadriceps, i.e., the front of the thigh.

[Page 40]
4. We guide our knees perfectly over our ankles - again, this provides us with a very stable and mechanically ideal position for hip extension. It may turn out that we will have problems with glute activation in such a version, and then I recommend gently guiding the knees wider, but do not guide them wider than over the little toes. When it is light, you can be tempted to spread them wider, but under a heavy load, it would be unwise and unsafe. An additional gadget (very fine at every level of advancement) will be a miniband placed on the knees. It is a very light band with a small circumference, ideal precisely for stronger glute activation. The hip thrust itself primarily engages our gluteus maximus muscles, but we also have the minimus and medius, which we best train precisely through such abduction with an imposed band - during hip thrusts we have all these functions activated at once!

5. The next point is the glutes and abs - I will repeat this element like a mantra in the subsequent exercises - we always tense both, this is the basis of our stabilization - the hardened center of our body. Before tensing and locking them, take care of proper pelvic positioning. We can have it in a neutral position or a slight posterior tilt (more on this in the "ground" section), which will eventually occur in the top phase, i.e., at full extension.

[Page 41]
Of course, we hold this tension throughout the entire movement. When we go down and lower the barbell, we will relax the glutes for a certain time, but the abs absolutely cannot be let go even for a second - we need constant tension of the entire anterior chain so as not to burden the spine. By the way, many people talk about burdening the spine through hip thrusts, but the truth is it works completely opposite - strengthening the glutes definitely relieves the spine in everyday use.

6. Now we will deal with the second fulcrum of our lever. First, let's establish what height to set the bench/stepper/box or whatever you'll be comfortably doing this exercise on. The bench should be set a few centimeters below our knee - roughly this height will ensure that the torso will be perpendicular to our lower legs, and the torso parallel to the floor. This will be the optimal position to lift the heaviest weights with optimally distributed forces. What if the bench is lower or higher? The lower the bench is, approaching even resting on the floor itself - the isolation of our glutes will increase, the work of other muscles will decrease, and unfortunately, wrist strain will grow and make it harder to maintain hip extension. A bench position that is too high will amplify the work of the front of our thighs and detract from the glutes' work. So let's trust gravity and set the body as it wishes - perpendicularly. We set the shoulder blades in retraction and depression - meaning we pull them back and pull them down. This position will give us much more stabilization in our back, but also in the entire torso.

7. We lean on the area of the inferior angles of the shoulder blades. But this is not the one and only mandatory position - you have to find your "spot", which will definitely be around these lower angles. The closer to the neck we lean, the harder it will be for us, and the closer to the hips, the easier it will be. Let's remember anyway that it's not about the highest possible weight number, but about the greatest possible overload of our muscles - and this is not 1:1 proportional to what's on the barbell. We place our hands on the barbell in a way that is comfortable and stable - my suggestion: an overhand grip, biacromial width, but without stressing - just wider than the shoulders - the actual distance of the barbell will actually dictate this distance for us.

[Page 42]
8. We move to the next point - we set the head. The head will be the biggest novelty here - we must look straight ahead - you could say at the barbell. Keep this head position; it will make it easier to tense the entire front of the body, ensure good rib positioning relative to the hips, and relieve the spine. That is, we unlearn the "straight" posture and in this exercise, we consciously hunch (Finally, right? They've been nagging us all our lives not to do it).

9. We start the movement from a dead stop - a deep breath with the Valsalva maneuver before we go up, and of course we finish with a full extension (ATTENTION! NOT HYPEREXTENSION!) of the hips - meaning we fly into a position where our torso is in one line with the thighs. We lower the barbell back to the floor and then we can exhale. We tense the glutes again to set the pelvis and don't let go of the abs, add another strong breath and go for the next rep. We also have a second option to perform this exercise - without lowering it to the floor, but just stopping right before. This option will provide greater muscle fatigue, but it may be a bit weaker for building strength. If we are beginners, I obligatorily recommend option number 1, but after a certain time of advancement, you can confidently test what will be the best version for us. I think I am leaving you, My Dear Ladies and My Dear Gentlemen, with the full knowledge of how to perform this exercise safely and effectively. BENCH PRESS
Okay, Gentlemen, so now we fly with the most important exercise for every beginner. How much on the bench? How much do you press? What goes on the chest? Heard that before? :D Well, I'll help you improve your answer to that question along with impeccable execution.
The bench press seems like an exercise for the "chest," but there is such a long kinematic chain here (very many body segments included in the movement) that it can be called a full-body exercise.

[Page 44]
1. Starting then from the feet - we want to keep them as close to our body as possible, narrow, additionally as close to the hips as possible. This will allow for powerful tension in the lower parts. We also have two options here:
- Greater stability - flat foot sole
- Greater tension - feet placed on toes
There isn't really a winner here, so I recommend choosing what "suits" you better. Of course, if we are not preparing for a bench press/powerlifting competition - depending on the federation, there are various specific requirements there. But this text is aimed at beginners, so for now, choose what's more comfortable for you.

2. The next element to pay attention to is the knees. We want to put our femurs into external rotation - we will achieve this by keeping our knees pointed outward while keeping our feet close together. This will provide a stable lower body and pre-tension the glutes - the largest muscles in our body, which lock everything in place, and give us the ability to move to the next step.

3. We want to keep the glutes in maximum tension - the knee positioning giving external rotation will already help us with this - this is one of the functions of our gluteal muscles. Additionally, with a conscious contraction, we squeeze these glutes even harder. Combining these three steps, we provide ourselves with absolute lower body stabilization. Only when we have secured all these elements can we take care of the torso and arms.

4. The next thing when tensing the glute is tensing the abs - you will see this in subsequent exercises. The combination of these two tensions sets our pelvis in the optimal position and maximally stabilizes it. This maneuver will also protect our spine in many exercises - one of them is the bench press. Despite lying down and pressing with our arms, our back is exposed to heavy overload if we do not take care of the correct pelvic positioning. Remember, Dear Viewer, that you must maintain all these tensions throughout the duration of the set.

[Page 45]
5. We move on to the elements that will have the most work to do. The upper back and the positioning of the shoulder blades is the most important area for stabilizing our shoulders and generally the entire torso - they close from above this entire tight cylinder we've created from our torso. Crucial for stabilization and safety in the shoulder complex is setting the shoulder blades in retraction and depression. This means in human terms: we pull the shoulder blades back and drop them down at the same time. This will give us a slight - or not so slight - arch in the back. Such an arch is nothing bad - on the contrary, it is a very healthy arch that matches the curvature of our spine, without which, during the bench press, we can incur serious health problems. Of course, an exaggerated arch won't be an optimal option either when it comes to "healthy pressing." Therefore, we should strive for a natural and comfortable curvature for us with retracted shoulder blades, tight abs, and glutes. The one that is comfortable for our spine will be optimal.

[Page 46]
6. Time to grab the barbell - because how long can you listen about things that aren't actually doing the work :/
Well, foundations are supposedly important - that's what we agreed on at the beginning, and the title of this elegant elaborate suggests it.

When it comes to the width of the barbell grip, we will look for the so-called biacromial width. How to measure it? - we measure the distance between our acromion processes and multiply it by 1.5 - this is the distance at which the index fingers wrapping the bar should be spaced. This will give us comfort, a good bar position on our body when lowering, and optimal movement to best transfer force to the barbell. The grip on the bar itself should be classic - with a closed thumb. This will give us peace of mind that the bar won't guillotine us. The barbell should be held in the hand as close to the base of the wrist as possible, because the larger this distance, the more overload on our wrist we will generate when pressing.

7. ARM RELATIVE TO THE TORSO - We do not guide the elbows perpendicular to the body. We want to have an angle between 45 and 70 degrees there. This will protect our shoulders from overload and will be a mechanically better position for pressing. So we kill two birds with one stone – we will be healthy and strong.

[Page 47]
The external rotation that we created in the femur will also be needed in the humerus (upper arm bone). When it comes to our arms, we activate this external rotation here by "screwing the elbows in" as if we wanted to break the barbell with our hands. This will anchor our arms in the shoulders and make them like steel.

8. The trajectory of the barbell's movement is such that we start above the shoulders, but we go down a bit towards the hips - let's say we lower it to the nipples. The destination spot on our body will actually be determined every time by grip width - the wider we grab, the higher on the chest we'll lower it. The narrower we grab, the closer to the hips it will be lowered. The elbow must be exactly under the wrist the whole time - hence this direction of guiding the weight.

[Page 48]
9. Practically the most important thing, and discussed at the very end - breathing.
We should take a breath before the repetition when the barbell is over our shoulders, and before starting the repetition, we should already be holding our breath so that no element of our torso moves. We go down and return with the barbell over the shoulders - only then is it time to exhale: a safe position where we can easily activate all our respiratory muscles. This maneuver of clenching the drawn-in air is called the Valsalva maneuver, and it is comprehensively applied in all compound exercises to maximize our body's capabilities and ensure good force transfer, while at the same time protecting our torso from any mechanical issues we might encounter while lifting heavy weights.

CLOSE-GRIP BENCH PRESS
The second crucial version of the press in plans for beginners will be pressing with a narrow grip width. There won't be much novelty here compared to what you read in the description of the "wide" press, but it definitely deserves a separate page. For people with a deep chest, i.e., people who have a large distance from the sternum to the spine, it may even turn out to be a better version of the press in terms of chest development than the wider one, even though the arm position seemingly causes less stretching. For such people, a large range of motion of the elbow relative to the torso will be much more important, which we won't experience by pressing with a wide grip - with a barbell, of course. For everyone, however, it will be a great complement to the bench press by strengthening the shoulders and triceps and additionally racking up training volume without overtiring our arm rotators, which have more work in a wider grip. Let the rotators rest, then, and let's get to work!

[Page 50]
1. The whole preparation will be identical to the regular bench press, and the only thing that will really change is the elbow path and the grip width, so we'll get straight to that.

2. This time we grab at an acromial width, that is, exactly above our shoulders - I recommend straightening your arms up while lying under the barbell and then sliding your hands down to grasp the bar. That will be our ideal shoulder-width grip. The rack should be set higher than with a wide grip, because it will be harder for us to unrack the barbell over our shoulders.

3. Elbows, as you (hopefully!) remember, are always guided under the barbell, so if we press with a narrow grip, we also guide the elbows narrowly. We will direct these elbows close to the ribs, unless we have shoulders like a massive four-door Gdansk wardrobe, then don't aim for the ribs, but simply keep the elbow under the wrist. The rest you already know exactly - a deep breath and exhale when we are safe at the top with the barbell over the shoulders, and we can pump with peace of mind!

SQUAT WITH BARBELL
The squat is a very versatile exercise, often mistaken for just a leg exercise. If we look deeper at the working muscles - it turns out we work with practically all our musculature. Muscles from the neck down to the feet are activated. With such exercises, it is very important to ensure proper technique and it is strictly necessary to check if we don't have posture defects. Some marginal curvatures won't do us great harm, but major defects can be troublesome for progression in the long run - see the first segment (the ground). If we are sure that we qualify to load our bodies - we get down to the technique, of course starting from the very bottom.

[Page 52]
1. In a perfect world, we would have our feet set parallel and narrow, hip-width apart. This would allow us the optimal base of support and the greatest possibilities to impose external rotation. In our world, however, it is our hips that will determine the width of our stance and the direction of our toes. If our hip sockets are directed outwards - a wider stance will be better, and if narrow and forward - a narrow stance with toes pointing forward will be better.
You can also check for yourself how to set these feet - (LINK TO VIDEO ON PROPER FOOT PLACEMENT)
Once we know what to do with the feet, we add an imposed force onto the floor with that foot as if we wanted to twist some button, but the foot remains in the same position. This imposes the force of external rotation on us - activating our glutes, which provides the beginning of optimizing our entire body setup before the squat.

2. The knees can stay calm for now - we will take care of them only when we start the movement, but I will mention now so you remember that they are not allowed to collapse inward relative to the feet.

[Page 53]
3. We move to the hips - here again, we must lock the entire system by combining the core-glute tensions.

4. The next element, classically, is the shoulder blades - in a clockwise motion, we bring them into depression and retraction. Unfortunately, if we don't want to fall into depression ourselves due to an injury, our shoulder blades must remain in constant depression. Once we have taken care of that, we can safely grab the barbell!

5. The width of our grip on the barbell will be determined by our shoulder mobility. The narrower we can do this, the more stable the "shelf" for the barbell will be - i.e., the space on the trapezius muscle.
I recommend, then, to grab it roughly like for the bench press, and then, once we unrack the barbell from the stand, try to gently bring our arms closer together until we achieve a comfortable, narrow, and stable position. It's important that nothing hurts us when we are in this "narrowest" position, and our arms are in line with the torso - elbows by the ribs.

[Page 54]
6. For full stabilization, we should add gently pulling the barbell "towards ourselves," as if we wanted to do a pull-up on this bar. This will ensure that the barbell won't lie loosely on our shoulders, but will become a very stable unity with us. This will add the necessary stiffness to the back by activating the latissimus dorsi and will eliminate the bar's mobility relative to the body.

7. The head is, as usual, an extension of the spine - a neutral, strong position without tilting up or down.

8. Before we start the squat, we have to unrack the barbell. We absolutely must do this in a squat position - feet together without any telemark pose. Unracking should practically be the first squat - more like a half-squat, of course, but with all the principles of a full squat maintained. Once we've stood up with it, we need to step back far enough to be at a safe distance from the rack, but short enough not to lose stabilization. Two well-executed steps should be enough - maximum three. Once we find our position, we activate the full sequence of tensing everything from the feet to the shoulders - and we go down!

[Page 55]
9. In the squat we will discuss here, it's about going straight down - we also have the low bar version, where we have to go down heavily with the hips, and the front squat version, where we go down heavily with the knees. In the high bar version, we fly exactly straight down. In an ideal world, this would mean exactly the same tilt from the ankles to the hips and knees (front and back), but we are built very differently, and in 100 cases of people there will be 100 different squats. The rule, in any case, is that we descend evenly from the knee and from the hip, maintaining as vertical a torso position as we are capable of.

10. Looking from the front - remember that the knees should not collapse inward relative to the foot throughout the entire movement. A good habit is to constantly push them outward right from the top, as soon as we begin the process of descending.

[Page 56]
11. Depth - we go as low as our build allows us to. Never forcefully deeper. Watch out for the "butt wink" (pelvis tucking under) and overly shallow squats. Thighs parallel to the floor will be a good minimum - the deeper from that with a straight back and good knee position, the better for us.

After every squat, remember to lock the movement at the top by newly tensing the glutes, and of course, maintain tension in the abs and arms on the barbell throughout the movement.
Half-squats seem best for sprinting and jumping, as the movement in this version most closely resembles the biomechanical work.
For muscle mass, a full squat (below 90 degrees) sounds the most sensible, as the range of motion and muscle activation increase. And then we have the deep squat, i.e., going as low as we can - for functionality and tissue health.
This is where we will best strengthen our ligaments, and it will also be a great option for people who have knee problems (contrary to what is mistakenly thought and said about squats). Additionally, don't worry about the knee going past the toe - you've probably heard more than once that it overloads the knees. Of course, it overloads the knees - just like the bench press overloads the chest. Thanks to this overload, our knees, chest, and whatever is subjected to training - are strengthened. It just has to be done intelligently - but more on that in the next segment of the book.

CONVENTIONAL DEADLIFT
There is no exercise that fries our nervous system more heavily than the title conventional deadlift. It causes forcing our body to generate extreme force in a very short time to lift a massive weight off the floor. In the case of the squat, such a lift is preceded by lowering the weight from a vertical position, which over a long time allows energy to accumulate before the upward concentric movement. We start the deadlift from a dead stop, from the floor, and we cannot accumulate any energy before going up. This sudden explosion of the nervous system provides us with such massive overloads. That is why advanced athletes can perform this exercise 2 or 3 times less frequently than, say, the squat. Well then, to the point - how is it done.

[Page 58]
1. The feet will optimally be placed again parallel, hip-width apart - exactly like in the squat (there will generally be a lot of similarities here). However, although this is the best position in terms of stabilization, it may turn out that we will be stronger in a slightly different setup. Nevertheless, initially, I recommend looking for this parallel position. Ideally, our shins should be as close to the barbell as possible - this will give us a mechanical advantage so that the bar won't have to be pulled towards us when lifting it to a vertical position.
You will arrive at a more personalized position yourselves - without my help.
*BELOW, EVERYTHING AS IN THE SQUAT*

2. The knees can stay calm for now - we will take care of them when we start the movement, but I will mention now so you remember that they are not allowed to collapse inward relative to the feet.

3. We move to the hips - here again, we must lock the entire system by combining the core-glute tensions.

4. The next element classically is the shoulder blades - in a clockwise motion, we bring them into depression and retraction. Unfortunately, if we don't want to fall into depression ourselves due to an injury, our shoulder blades must remain in constant depression. Once we have taken care of that, we can safely grab the barbell!

[Page 59]
5. We grab the barbell slightly wider than shoulder-width or at shoulder-width - wide enough so that the knees have room not to collapse inward, but narrow enough to be as close to a vertical position as possible. Once we grab the barbell, we add external rotation of the arms - exactly the same as in the bench press. We impose a motion as if we wanted to break the barbell by rotating our arms outward.

6. The head is, as usual, an extension of the spine - a neutral, strong position without tilting up or down.

7. This is how it should all look now before we pull the barbell up. Our body from the side will line up very differently depending on the length of individual segments - shins, thighs, torso, arms - but there is one important rule that must be maintained. The knee, hip, and shoulders from the side should line up in a triangle in the order listed from bottom to top. Meaning the hips above the knees and the shoulders above the hips. If we cannot get our body into this position - we will probably have to adapt either a different version of the deadlift to our levers or look for a completely different substitute for torso and hip extension - e.g., hip thrust, hyper-extensions on a Roman chair, etc. In any case, the more horizontal our torso is, the more the back (and the back of our legs) will be involved in the movement, and the more vertical it is, the more work our legs will have (especially the front). We achieve this position through full tension of the posterior chain - meaning we go down to the barbell, and then we look for a position in which all the muscles on the back of our body will be maximally taut. This is our deadlift position and when we find it - we can go up!

[Page 60]
8. Before we go up, we need to pull the slack out of the bar so that we don't crash it into the plates. This will protect us from the dangerous forces acting on the spine. If we pull the slack, there will be no sound when going up – and that is exactly what we are looking for. A good comparison is a car being towed on a rope - imagine the towing car suddenly taking off before the rope tightens. With such a sudden jerk, the tow rope can very easily snap.

9. Deep breath, Valsalva maneuver, and then we can blast off upwards.
We start the movement with hip work - meaning you could say the shoulders and back will go up first. It's as if we wanted to squeeze our gluteal muscles with all our might and stand up using that force. If we make a mistake and start working by straightening our legs - our knees will move back and the overload will shift to the spine (spinal erectors) - we will create an unfavorable lever. We focus on the work of the hips, and then all the other segments activate until we reach full torso extension - we then pay attention not to hyperextend the torso. If we are already at the top and squeeze the glute hard, keeping the abs tight right from the bottom, we will be safe and won't go past vertical. We guide the barbell very close to our legs throughout the movement, and finally, our hips. Every centimeter of deviation will make the exercise harder for us. Of course, when the weight gets big enough, we won't even have a choice, but initially, we can operate it relatively freely, so it's good to pay attention to this. Activating the lats, we pull the barbell to our body - not just upward. We finish the movement in full torso extension with shoulder blades dropped down and back. Ideally, we would take a breath and exhale when we are at the bottom, but the deadlift is often so tiring that this will be needed at the moment of extension at the top. If this will be needed for you too, then at this very moment exhale and take another breath, and only then do we begin the eccentric phase (lowering the barbell to the ground). We go down exactly the same way we went up, i.e., we start the movement with the hips - by shifting the hips back and leaning the torso forward, and gradually going down, we add the knees to it so that we don't hit them with the barbell. Of course, the same rule as when lifting - barbell as close to the torso as possible. Once we lower the barbell to the floor, we can exhale, after which we take another breath and can then perform the next repetition.
There is also a second method of performing the deadlift, the so-called "touch and go".

[Page 61]
Then it looks a bit like we're playing basketball with the barbell :D, meaning we also lower it all the way to the floor, but there is no moment to inhale and exhale, we just "bounce" it off the floor - right after contact with the floor, we lift it again. With this method, you can do significantly more volume, although for beginners I definitely do not recommend it, because it is not easy to focus well on proper technique, and every such hit throws you off the ideal position and tension.

BARBELL BENT-OVER ROW
There is no other back exercise as good as the bent-over barbell row. Not only do we concentrically and eccentrically train the lats, teres muscles, rhomboids, and trapezius, but we also work isometrically with our entire spinal erector. Back aside - the rest of our posterior chain is also activated and our legs also work hard to survive this heavy position in which we row. If I've hopefully convinced you why it's worth rowing, we'll move on to the technique broken down to the atoms.

[Page 63]
1. The larger the base of support we create on straight (wall) legs - the more stable we will be - you already know this from previous exercises, if you read how to do them. But here it is even more important than in any previous exercise. Well, and foot placement isn't dictated by the structure of our pelvis and hips and mobility like in the squat and deadlift. Here we must focus solely on being as stable as possible.

2. Legs together with the torso in full tension vertically. So we squeeze the glutes, abs, shoulder blades in depression and retraction, head as an extension of the torso. Everything that is most important in our setup will only start once we grab the barbell. I choose the underhand version for beginners, as it is the strongest arm position when gripping the barbell.

3. Every exercise in which we grab the barbell underhand we will grab at shoulder width or a bit wider - this is the so-called acromial grip - at the width of the acromion processes. However, if you choose an overhand grip - it will definitely be wider than shoulder width. A good indicator of correctly chosen width will be the arm position. If we guide the forearms vertically in the sagittal plane (the one as if an arrow shot through our head), with an underhand grip, exactly shoulder width should allow us such a trajectory.
Once we know the grip width, we grab the barbell, close the grip, and can lift it - exactly like in the deadlift if we are lifting from the floor.
If we are lifting from a rack, then no problem: just a step back and we are ready. Wherever we pick up the barbell from - first we stand up fully straight and only then lower ourselves into position.

4. We lower ourselves "from the hips" - meaning we go down not by bending the knees (we only gently bend the knees), but we push the butt back and lower the shoulders heavily. Now the question is: how low to go? If we want to maximize the work of the lats...

[Page 64]
...meaning, let's call it, work on "width", we lower ourselves as close to a horizontal torso position as possible. If we want to work on the upper back to have ABS (Absolute Badass Shoulders/No Neck), we will row closer to a vertical position, but generally, I don't recommend this version to any beginners. My suggestion for those starting with the sport to engage as many back muscles as possible and train them hardest with a relatively comfortable body position... Is to lower yourself to 45 degrees. Then the work is distributed equally between the lats and trapezius (and of course a mass of other smaller back muscles).

5. Once we find ourselves in our ideal rowing position, all that's left is to row. Here we will focus on two aspects - the shoulder blade and the arm.
When it comes to shoulder blades, we again have two options to choose from - we can keep them constantly in retraction and depression and row only with our arms - then we maximize the work of the lats. The second version, however, is that we pull the shoulder blades together before every rowing motion and relax them after every lowering of the barbell. This will add a lot of work to the trapezius and rhomboids, but we probably won't be able to perform the same volume by adding this second variant. I usually recommend holding the shoulder blades in one position because the torso angle in rowing already forces more upper back work. Usually, it's the lats that are neglected in the average person who is just starting to train.

[Page 65]
6. Our arms will essentially be guided by gravity, but until the weight gets reasonably heavy, we must focus on pulling the barbell vertically upwards ourselves.

7. The underhand rowing position will guide us towards bringing it close to the thighs and hips on every attempt.
The stomach is not the finish line we must reach. It's our shoulder blade and torso that determine the endpoint of the row. If the elbow passes the line of the back, we stop working with the lats, so we don't necessarily have to wrestle with that final range of motion. I would rather suggest doing more work in the "strong" range of motion.

At this point, Dear Viewer, it seems you know everything most crucial about the bent-over barbell row, so I cordially invite you to pump up those wings! STANDING BARBELL OVERHEAD PRESS
Definitely my favorite exercise. I have a lot of sentiment for it and a score to settle - I haven't managed to press 100kg :D
The best exercise for long-term progression of overloads on our deltoids, upper chest, triceps, traps, and of course whole-body stabilization through a very long lever.
The longest kinematic chain in a straight line that we are capable of creating with our body appears precisely in this exercise. It is called the "true" press. The bench press is a short and easy movement in an easy lying position. Here we must stabilize the entire body from the ankles to the wrists, which we have high above our heads with an added load. This requires a lot of isometric work from us, but you'll find out everything step by step in a moment.
Let's fly...

[Page 67]
1. The larger the base of support we create on straight (wall) legs, the more stable we will be - you already know this from previous exercises if you read how to perform them. But here it is even more important than in any previous exercise. And foot placement isn't dictated by pelvis and hip structure and mobility like in the squat and deadlift. Here we must focus solely on being as stable as possible.

2. The knees absolutely do not participate in this exercise. You can sometimes see at gyms how people use leg drive when pressing weight overhead - this is a different exercise - the so-called "push press". We, on the other hand, do a static version, when it comes to the lower body, of this exercise. Only the shoulder girdle works here on transferring the weight overhead. We lock them in extension, tight quads (front of thigh) and glutes of course with all our might - and I will just remind you that it stays this way throughout the whole movement, otherwise with a weak foundation the whole structure will crumble - slight external rotation imposed by the feet again won't hurt. Of course, whenever we tense the glute, we also keep the abs tight - you already know that. Tight abs and glute are like yin and yang, light and shadow, Saker and Injuries. They just always appear together. In the standing press there is one key difference - we strictly hold this tension in both places through the entireeeee movement - we never relax. During the movement we will make a slight movement in the hips, but more on that later.

3. We hold the shoulder blades here as in other exercises in depression and retraction. As it was already said, so that we don't end up in depression ourselves due to injuries, we have to accept that the shoulder blades will have a sad life in our workouts. Although once we actually perform the press, this will change a bit, but more on that in a second.

[Page 68]
4. The head in the standing press is the only exercise where it will be mobile.
We start with a straight neck and a tucked chin - head in retraction, but as we push the barbell up, the head must make way so the barbell can travel in a straight vertical line, rather than bypassing the chin, nose, and forehead. So while the barbell moves off our collarbones, we gently tilt the head back along with the hips pushed slightly forward, and we can comfortably press.

5. Let's get to the barbell!
The grip will be determined by our mobility and levers - the length of the humerus and forearm bones. In any case, we want to grab as narrow as we can, maintaining a vertical forearm perpendicular to the barbell. For most, it will probably be slightly wider than shoulder width. Looking from the side, the most important thing is that the elbows are exactly under the barbell or even slightly in front of it. This isn't an intuitive arm position, but even though it will seem weird at first - it will give us the best mechanism to press overhead. We should rest the barbell as close to our neck as possible, if our mobility and arm segment lengths allow it. The further we hold it from the neck, the worse for our result and the less safely we'll lower it during the eccentric phase.

6. We grab the barbell as close to the base of the wrist as possible. The closer to the base we grab, the better the transfer of the force we generate, and the lesser the overload for the wrist itself - a better lever.

[Page 69]
7. To be able to press before the barbell passes our chin, we must do three things. First: a deep breath with the Valsalva maneuver. Only then do we gently push the hips forward - but a cardinal error would be bending the back (deepening the lumbar lordosis) or bending the knees. We keep the quads, glutes, and abs tight, but push the hips forward - this will clear a vertical path for the barbell a bit, but if we add tilting the head back at this moment, we create a safe space and won't worry about catching on anything. Once the barbell passes our hairline, we simultaneously align our body back into a straight line.
Regarding the trajectory of the movement, it's a perfectly vertical movement if executed correctly, but it will feel like we are pressing a bit behind ourselves. Remember that the barbell should be pressed overhead, not behind the head. The head is supposed to slide under the barbell when it's already up, not the barbell going over the head while it's tilted back. At the beginning, I recommend learning to perform these movements sequentially before doing them simultaneously. When I teach someone to do the OHP, I always ask for this sequence: hips -> head -> barbell | AND DOWN -> barbell -> head -> hips. After a few workouts, clients start performing these movements simultaneously on their own without my asking.

8. When we begin lowering the barbell, we do exactly the same sequence, just in reverse. And remember to lower it as close to the neck as possible. The further we push it away, the worse lever we will create. Of course, we exhale only after we rest the barbell in its place at the bottom - on the collarbones. Keeping full-body tension the whole time, we take another breath and can safely start the next repetition.

UNDERHAND PULL-UP (CHIN-UP)
As Robercik Dynamit says: "By the way a guy does pull-ups, I can judge how he is in bed." It's probably obvious now what a very important exercise this is in this set. Focus hard on your technique in case you run into Robur at the gym. The underhand pull-up is much more than a back exercise - no surprise there by now. While lat pulldowns are exactly that, on the pull-up bar, we work practically with our whole body. It's said anyway that there is no better back exercise, but then again, the same is basically said about rowing. I suggest less philosophizing and just doing both.

[Page 71]
1. We stabilize the body exactly the same as before the standing overhead press. Actually, it will be practically the reverse movement, except we won't be standing on the floor.
Before hanging on the bar, let's focus on these things:
tight glutes and abs, shoulder blades in retraction and depression, head as an extension of the spine - the rest is irrelevant at the moment, because in a second we will not be in contact with the floor.

2. We grab the bar at the exact same width as the close-grip bench press and bent-over row - shoulder-width (acromial) with an underhand grip, of course, so palms facing our face. Once we grab the bar and hang, we need to do a few important things before going up.

[Page 72]
3. If the space under the bar allows it, we keep our legs straight in one line with our torso. If we want to be perfect, we can borrow the foot position from gymnastics - plantar flexion - toes pointing to the floor. This will help a lot with torso stabilization when pulling over the bar - it prevents the body from swinging.

4. With shoulder blade work, we again have two options to choose from - just like with rowing. Of course, the key before the first movement is to pull the shoulder blades hard back and down. We perform the full pull-up motion, lower ourselves all the way down, and again release the shoulder blades so they elevate (then our body obviously drops).
Here again, we will significantly increase the work of the lower trapezius - the neglected part, because everything we hold in our hands or even a backpack we wear - activates its upper part. The lower part is often dormant, and that's why it's worth paying attention to.
The second version is that after pulling the shoulder blades together, we keep this retracted position throughout the whole movement. This will definitely be better when the weights increase significantly - it's simply a safer version. For lighter training phases or simply a lower percentage of your one-rep max, I would definitely do the version with elevation and depression.

5. Often the criteria for a completed pull-up is the so-called "chin over the bar." You definitely had tasks like this in PE class. Often you'll have to comply with these requirements. But in our training, we should perform exercises within a healthy full range of motion - in the underhand pull-up, such a range is the moment when the elbow reaches the lats, i.e., roughly the upper arm aligning with the torso. For many people, at this point, the chin will not yet be above the bar, and it is nothing bad that...

[Page 73]
...it will be much lower. This can be decided by, for instance, a long forearm bone and a short upper arm. Sticking the chin out for a person built like this will be hard and even unhealthy (long-term).

6. I'll just remind you for dessert that here too we care about the "Valsalva" - we inhale at the bottom before the movement and exhale again in that same position after completing a full rep.

You probably know everything you need about pull-ups at this stage, but classically I refer you to the video material for the overachievers (they are the best!): youtu.be/olxgbxHIHTY

DIPS ON PARALLEL BARS
Dips, dipsy, pompki na poręczach - whatever you call them, they will be just as good an exercise. Dips are said to be the squat for the upper body. Why are exercises where we move the body through space (like the dip and pull-up) so good compared to exercises where the body is static and an external object is moved? There are theories that a colossal amount more stabilizing muscles are working, and hence the incredible physique development from precisely these versions. Convinced? Let's get to the technique!

[Page 75]
1. Like in any multi-joint exercise - we will stabilize the body from bottom to top here - identical to the pull-up, so I refer you to the stabilization in that chapter.

2. When we are ready, we step onto the platform by the parallel bars and grab them.

3. If we have the ability to adjust the width, let's pick a width so that our shoulders, elbows, and wrists are in one line. If we don't, at least ensure that the elbows are over the wrists - this is the most important and absolutely crucial regardless of the width. Let's keep it brief with the grip because, as standard, we will start from the bottom up.

4. What about the legs then? If the height of the bars allows, we keep the legs straight throughout the movement without any bending or tilting in any direction. Toes pointed, if we want to add the cherry on top. Glutes and abs constantly tight and ready through the whole set, even before we start doing the dips. I hope you remember about the head and I don't have to remind you, but just in case, I'll add - extension of the torso. We don't hunch and we don't look at the ceiling. Strong, stiff position through the whole set. Breaking this position puts our levator scapulae muscle in an unhealthy position and in the long run can breed a lot of shoulder problems, especially in an exercise as demanding as dips.

[Page 76]
5. How low to go? There is no constant, single point we should descend to, but a good goal is to drop the shoulder to elbow level or slightly below. This can be compared to depth in the squat - there, too, it is best to go below 90 degrees. There is no point in forcing yourself deep, but if you feel that lower will be safe and comfortable, a larger range of motion is always our friend - again, like in the squat.

6. We inhale and exhale always exclusively when our arms are straight - when we are at the top. Breathing during the exercise can result in issues with the sternum and ribs, although these problems can be caused by something else - more on that in the next point.

A frequent problem during dips is clicking or even pain in the sternum.
This is caused by inflammation of the rib cartilages that attach to the sternum, or even their detachment and mobility in this area. The most frequent cause of such a problem is a lack of control when descending - so-called divebombing and bouncing out of the bottom - instead of using muscle strength, we shift the work onto ligaments, cartilage, and tendons. The best solution will be, first, visiting a physiotherapist and, unfortunately, a break from training! Just so these poor little ribs and sternum can rest from continuous work. You can also focus on strengthening the serratus anterior muscle and improving chest mobility - more on that in the video: youtu.be/yodXoqPPCvo

DUMBBELL LATERAL RAISES
Finally, we have arrived at a less complicated exercise, but oh so important for our physique. Aspiring bodybuilders - listen very closely now! Lateral raises (in various forms) are the best exercise for building shoulder width. It is exactly this medial (not lateral - no such thing) head that makes us look like we lift when facing forward in clothes. There are many dangers lurking here, however, so non-bodybuilders, please focus hard as well.
Lateral raises are the first isolation exercise on our list. For this reason, it does not require full-body stabilization, but it definitely requires local work (of our shoulder girdle), meaning it's worth eliminating the work of the hips, legs, etc. The less we use the rest of the body, the less weight we'll need to train the deltoid muscles.

[Page 78]
1. We sit on a bench (even the bench press one), keep our legs in front, knees together, which will give us room to lower the dumbbells freely.
We will need slight external rotation in the arm for safety (more on that in a second), so we lean our torso forward (we don't sit vertically, but tilted).
How much to lean, we'll explain at the very end, but for now, I'm planting the seed that such a position is key.
The starting position is simply arms alongside the torso, before we start the movement, again I strongly emphasize the shoulder blades - pulled back and down. We hold this position of the shoulder blades sturdily through the whole movement. If we relax them - a lot of the work of raising the arm will be taken over by the upper trapezius - which, as I mentioned, already gets too much work.

2. The end position is arms perpendicular to the torso - forming a T-shape. We don't cross the shoulder line, because again - this amplifies upper trapezius work.

[Page 79]
3. In the transverse plane, we pay attention to how far forward the arm is relative to the torso, and the position of the elbows. We guide our arms in the scapular plane, meaning not straight out to the sides, but about 15-20 degrees forward. When it comes to elbow flexion, the case is open, but beware: the greater the elbow flexion, the smaller the load will be on our deltoids, and the larger the load on our rotators. So a slight bend in the elbow should be the best option for us, 5-10 degrees.

4. To make this exercise completely safe, we must do one more thing - this is exactly why we leaned forward. Adding external rotation without leaning the body would engage the front delt more strongly, and we don't need that in isolation exercises. The rotation itself opens up the subacromial space and prevents any possibility of tendon damage – in short: it protects our shoulder.

I think that's it for now - just in case, I refer you to a sensational material we recorded with Doctor Biernat, where there's more on lateral raises and more: youtu.be/wzbX9cDIGDS

REVERSE LUNGES
Our glutes love exercises where one leg does a lot of work, and the other just helps it a little bit. That's when all three of their parts (gluteus maximus, medius, and minimus) develop best.
You could say reverse lunges are a single-leg squat, but that would be slightly misleading. It's actually on two legs, just in a "split" stance. But to be even more precise, it's one and a half legs, because one stabilizes more and the other works. However you want to classify it, reverse lunges are a sensational addition to the squat, not loading the spine to such a great extent and not requiring as much mobility of individual body parts. Up to the point of unracking the barbell and getting ready for the movement, everything looks like a classic squat, so let's agree, Dear Viewer, that I sent you there, and here we'll move right to the movement itself.

[Page 81]
1. As the name suggests (in Polish 'zakrok'), we perform the lunge backward, meaning it differs from a forward lunge in that we step the leg to the back. Of course, throughout the entire movement, we ensure the barbell maintains a horizontal position.

2. Important thing when stepping back with the leg - make sure the legs remain hip-width apart, meaning we step straight back in line. Similarly, make sure the ankle doesn't collapse sideways on the rear leg.

3. When it comes to the angles of leg bends, the ideal option is to drop down to such a leg span that the shin of the front leg goes slightly forward (over the toes). This will give us the mechanically best position for the heaviest weight and greatest overload. We can also maximize hip work, and minimize knee (and quad) work, by using a wider stance, which will make the shin stay fully vertical, and we will observe the most bend in our hips. I recommend the first option for beginners. Even if glutes are our priority.

4. After every reverse lunge, we return to the standing vertical position, and in the next repetition, we switch the working leg - meaning this time the other goes back.
I'll just note at the end - for beginner ladies, before you work with a barbell, it might be worth starting with a bodyweight version. Only after building stability and strength would I recommend grabbing a smaller barbell (about 10 kg) or dumbbells held simply by your sides.
I don't have a video to refer you to, but if one pops up, it will certainly be added. And to you, My Dear Lady/Gentleman, I wish successful lunging.

PLANK
We have waded through most of the exercises. What's left are basically ones that care mainly for our core, meaning the abs and the whole area around them, called the muscular corset. It is a whole mass of larger and smaller muscles that were constantly working in most of the exercises we performed described above. But because they are so very crucial for our body, it's definitely worth devoting a little more time and interest to them - so that we become indestructible weightlifting machines! Let's move on to the first of them, the plank.

[Page 83]
1. Before we set up in the plank, let's go back to absolute basics, so we get on all fours, except the knees aren't under the hips, but slightly further back. We aren't leaning on straight arms either, but on the elbows - which already at this point should be directly under our shoulders. We should choose this position so that when we straighten our legs, we are a perfect board with elbows under shoulders and a straight foot in a 90-degree position relative to the rest of the leg. It will be roughly the position like in the photo.

2. Returning to the foundations of our body - we keep the feet hip-width apart with heels either vertical or turning slightly inward to increase glute activation.

3. Once we raise ourselves up, of course, the whole body stays in this tension scheme like during other multi-joint exercises. Legs straight, tight glutes and abs with a neutrally positioned pelvis - in a plank, we can optionally have it in a slight posterior tilt, which will enhance the work of our abdominal muscles. Of course, the torso is also straight. Shoulder blades in the legendary depression and retraction - and watch out here, it will be very hard due to the compression caused by the floor. Therefore, this will be one of the most important elements to pay attention to. The head - I will repeat to boredom - is an extension of the spine, but it will do you good if you read it 30 times. That will mean "I guess there's something to it, damn, since he keeps bringing it up..."

[Page 84]
4. The arms, as you already know, we keep perfectly under the shoulders. But let's look from the front - here too, arms shoulder-width apart. Simple architecture - if we want a building to stand a long time, everything must be perpendicular to the millimeter. It's the same case with the plank. Arms and legs must be perfect walls to hold up such a heavy and long ceiling as we create with our body.

5. We'll be tempted to keep our forearms close together. Sure! Even clasping hands in prayer for the set to end faster. But beware of this, because it introduces internal rotation to our shoulders, which is not necessarily beneficial for us. Either straight ahead, or if we want to go any direction, it's outward.

6. A good method for quickly grasping this position - as well as the rest of the exercises with such stabilization - is using a broomstick. If you have a training partner or are not ashamed to bother other gym users, they can place such a stick on your back. Your task at this moment will be to make sure the stick touches only and exclusively: the back of the head, the shoulder blade area (the peak of kyphosis) and the sacrum/glutes, if we have them tight enough, it should probably be there! Making sure these points constantly touch the stick will force us to hold the optimal plank position.

CABLE CRUNCHES (ALLAHS)
The second exercise in which we pay attention to our corset is the so-called Allahs. More mundanely, I'd call them crunches with an upper cable pulley, but "Allahs" sounds so cool! In this exercise, the dominant role is taken over by the rectus abdominis muscles, i.e., those "cool", "pretty", "sexy" ones, and the ones everyone hopes for taking their first step into the gym. Of course, they are very important, not only because they look so impressive at low body fat. Especially if we have deadlifts and other posterior chain exercises. We cannot forget about the anterior chain, and Allahs are one of the better versions for training it - it's easy to progress here and we get a nice range of motion under tension. To the point:

[Page 86]
1. Most often, this exercise is performed in a kneeling position with high hips. Such a position will provide us with a good station for flexing and extending the spine - because this is exactly the movement we are looking for when we want to train the external abdominal muscles. A good cue is bringing the ribs closer to the pelvis or chest to hips, and of course, in the eccentric movement, moving them apart. This will be important when it comes to the start and end positions, but first we'll deal with the arms and head.

2. We adjust the cable handle to our preferences - anyway, I recommend the "steering wheel" (V-bar/handle) grip. It seems most comfortable for most trainees. I also often see the use of "braids" (rope), but this is not the optimal version because our grip in such a position won't handle a weight suitable for the abs.
Whichever grip we choose, the most important rule is: the arms relative to the head do not change place in space. I recommend pulling the handle to the top of your head or the back of your neck depending on where it will be more comfortable and how low we will go in the movement.

3. We know all the fixed points in the exercise, so we can start the first rep. We start by grabbing the handle and dropping into a kneeling position. We lift the hips high and set the spine in hyperextension so as to pre-stretch the abdominal muscles. But we don't stretch to exaggeration. It's simply about starting from a stretched position. We tense the abs and start bringing the ribs closer to the hips (another good cue is elbows to knees).

[Page 87]
4. This is roughly what the completed movement should look like, and I recommend staying in this end position for a moment. Let's say you stay in it for about a second and only then the return to the top occurs.

5. When it comes to breathing, we will achieve the best abdominal muscle contraction by exhaling during the concentric movement. Meaning as we go down with elbows to knees, we slowly exhale the air, and inhale again as we approach the top position - the starting one.

Y-RAISE (TRAP RAISE)
The lower part of the trapezius muscle is neglected by gravity. Whatever we grab in our hands - we load the upper part of this muscle. The lower works completely oppositely to the upper, so if, for example, we grabbed something overhead and pulled it towards ourselves - only then would we start working with this muscle. We also work it when we bring the arm overhead - it rotates the shoulder blade. And we will utilize this exact function in the Y-raise exercise.
The "Y" comes from the position our arms assume relative to the torso in the position of full muscle contraction.

[Page 89]
1. We start by grabbing the cable rope or dumbbells, lying on a slight incline bench.
If we do this exercise standing, we stabilize as usual. Even lying down, despite the unusual position.
The legs are less important because the kinematic chain ends at the shoulder girdle, but nevertheless, the torso remains active even while resting on the bench.
We squeeze the glutes and abs, and the head of course remains an extension of the spine. Before starting the arm movement, we bring the shoulder blades into retraction and depression.
Only when we set our shoulders this way can we start working. We keep the arms straight at the elbow joint and in a hammer position - palms facing each other.
Arms at a 60-degree angle - to form a Y.

2. Arms in such a position with tight shoulder blades are raised until they are in a straight line with our torso. We stay in the end position for a moment. We can also apply the Valsalva maneuver here, but it is not strictly necessary.

FACEPULL
An exercise that combines the training of the external rotators of our arms, rear deltoids, and upper back. It is one of the best exercises for improving a hunched posture in protraction. It will also take care of our shoulder health, as it will improve the balance of strength in most people. I believe a separate exercise for these parts should be included in everyone's plan. Let's get down to business.
Your tool for this exercise can be a cable pulley, a resistance band, or dumbbells.
Whatever you choose, the principle is identical - only the plane changes if you choose dumbbells. With dumbbells, we perform this exercise lying prone on a bench. Let's move then to the execution itself.

[Page 91]
1. We start with relaxed shoulder blades and arms straight out in front.
We initiate the movement by pulling the shoulder blades (arms remain straight of course) into depression and retraction. Once they are in the right place, we add pulling the arms toward the face - hence the name facepull. We lift the hands high, while the elbows go low and wide.

2. We guide the elbows at shoulder height widely, while we try to guide the hands as high as possible to gain maximum external rotation. You could say we aim the hands at forehead level. It's best if we stop briefly in the end position so as not to use momentum, and then slowly return. We perform the return in reverse order, meaning first the arms, then we release the shoulder blades until the arms are straight and in protraction.

3. In the resistance band version, the hands will probably be closer to each other because the band will kind of force it, but that's nothing bad. We can also try to grab the band wider and do it similarly to the cable pulley.
In the dumbbell version, I suggest the bench raised to 15 degrees or completely flat, if we will be able to completely hang our arms down.

BEGINNER
I present to you an updated, slightly improved training plan, already popular for a few years on the web as "SAKER'S FBW", exactly for those who are just starting at the gym now. The last update was in 2016 and since then I practically wouldn't change anything - I only added two exercises that add flavor and are the cherries (in my opinion) on a quite tasty cake.

The training plan for beginners is characterized by a low repetition range with a significant number of sets, which are ideal for training the nervous system in technical efficiency. If we want to learn something quickly, we should do it short and often - that is why you see 5x5 the most in it, i.e., 5 sets of 5 repetitions.

Both women and men have their version of the plan and it does not differ in these main 5x5 exercises, but it differs slightly in accessories. All these exercises that you see in both training versions can be found thoroughly described in the second segment of the book.

We perform the training three times a week - for recovery reasons and because of protein synthesis, which we utilize best by training roughly every 48 hours to quickly develop muscle mass. So in practice, such a training looks as follows:
Monday A
Wednesday B
Friday A
Monday B
Wednesday A
Friday B

Once a week we take a 72-hour break. I suggest the weekends, because for most people it will probably just be convenient. But a longer break can be implemented anywhere in the week - as long as it suits you!

It's great if we manage to add weight every workout in every exercise. My proposal is: Deadlift and Squat +5 kg, rest of exercises 5x5 +2.5 kg.
For women accordingly 2.5 kg, and in 5x5 +1 kg (i.e., 0.5 kg per side on the barbell).
We do not change the plan until stagnation, meaning we follow exactly this scenario every workout until we fail. When such stagnation happens to us, the first step is to take 10% off the weight and progress again from this 90% of what it was.

[Page 95]
For example: if we reached 100 kg 5x5 and failed, on the next workout we put on 90 kg and do 5x5 again. We can do such adjustments for some time, but when it becomes almost constant, we have to switch to the intermediate plan - see the next point.

Arms: free choice - I do not impose anything specific, just to make it more interesting. I allow you to choose one isolation exercise for biceps and triceps yourselves, preferably one that will allow you to feel the muscle, and nothing else will hurt - especially the elbow, wrist, shoulder, etc. As for the "calves" exercise, here again a free choice, as long as you perform the exercise with a straight leg at the knee - i.e., e.g., standing or on a leg press. We cannot be bent over like in seated calf raises.

INTERMEDIATE
When the ability to progress from workout to workout ends, the best thing we can do in the context of further physique development will be moving to juggling repetition ranges over the span of a week. Thanks to this - again like a beginner - we will be able to disrupt homeostasis every 48 hours. Once we do mainly strength, once hypertrophy, and once strength endurance. Three different adaptations over the span of a week that do not strongly interfere with each other. When we are intermediate, we should already understand very well how everything works, so this plan is really just a skeleton that each of you should arrange for yourselves to suit your weak links/weak body parts. Basic exercises are listed in such a way that the push/pull chain balance matches reasonably well. Additionally, a few accessory exercises are selected for each of the workouts in the week. In accessories, of course, we select exercises to suit our own weak points. As for the basic exercises, for example, if we have bilateral push - we choose high bar squat / low bar squat / front / leg press / other leg press versions. Vertical pull - any form of pull-up / lat pulldown / other exercise where we pull something vertically down. I would lay out the training days exactly as they are numbered in terms of sequence.
